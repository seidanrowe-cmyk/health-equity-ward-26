Root Economic Drivers

What's actually driving it

Four conditions in the ward's economy, and the health outcome each one produces.

01Food access

The corner store sets the menu

About one in five residents here faces food insecurity, well above the national rate. Fresh produce means a bus transfer; the corner store sells cheap, sodium-heavy calories within walking distance. Residents eat what the neighborhood prices within reach, and blood pressure follows.

Outcome: hypertension · diabetes
02Housing

Rent crowds out prescriptions

Median household income sits near $60k while rents climb in from the Logan Square side. Families spend the margin holding the apartment, then skip the copay and the refill. A near-miss on eviction raises blood pressure by itself.

Outcome: deferred care · chronic stress
03Coverage

Residents skip refills to save money

Sinai's community survey found 1 in 4 residents went without a needed prescription because of cost. High blood pressure gives no warning before the stroke. The neighborhood then pays for dialysis and cardiac admissions that a $4 pill would have prevented.

Outcome: stroke · kidney failure
04Public dollars

The city bought incarceration here

Public spending on incarceration in Humboldt Park has run to hundreds of millions, among the highest neighborhood totals in Chicago. Those same dollars could have funded clinics, jobs, or housing. Residents pay the difference in years of life.

Outcome: compounding disinvestment
These are economic conditions expressed as blood pressure.
The case for treating health policy as economic policy
Strategic Policy Levers

Three levers a ward office can pull

Ward-scale, fundable now, each with the economic case attached.

1

Put produce where people already shop

Menu money · Division Street corridor +

Direct menu and participatory-budget dollars to produce refrigeration in existing corner stores and a longer farmers' market season on Division. Residents already walk to these stores every week. Stock them with what a cardiologist would want on the shelf.

The economic caseOne stroke costs tens of thousands in acute care, and Humboldt Park Health absorbs much of that bill uncompensated. Produce subsidy costs a few hundred dollars per household per year. Fund the cheap end of that ledger.
2

Bring blood-pressure checks to trusted places

Libraries · churches · the fieldhouse +

Pay community health workers to run walk-in screenings and medication check-ins at libraries, churches, and the fieldhouse. Some residents will never enter a clinic, so meet them where they already spend their week. Controlling blood pressure takes a cuff, a pill, and someone who follows up.

The economic caseCommunity health worker programs return more than they cost in avoided ER visits. Each controlled patient means one fewer stroke, dialysis start, or cardiac admission. The wages go to hires from the neighborhood.
3

Treat housing stability as health infrastructure

Tenant legal aid · anti-displacement policy +

Fund tenant legal aid, deconversion protection, and affordable set-asides near the park. A family that keeps its home keeps its clinic, its pharmacy, and its routines. A family pushed out loses all three and restarts care from zero in a neighborhood that doesn't know them.

The economic caseHousing precarity raises chronic stress, and chronic stress raises blood pressure. Stability pays out twice: families keep spending at businesses on Division and North Avenue, and the health system skips the cost of re-diagnosing them somewhere else.
